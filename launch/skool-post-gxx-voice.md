It's called The Continuity Bible. 43 pages, one scene of six shots, and the whole thing exists because of a
single question: what has to survive the cut?

Three layers, and that's it:

IDENTITY — what must never change. Five face anchors, five fixed landmarks, a time band.

VARIANT — every change you made on purpose, with a code, a cause and an end. V2 cardigan open. P-01b mug
tipped. L2 lamp off.

SHOT STATE — what is true in this shot: action, where the body is, what the hands hold, framing, and one
handoff line saying what the next shot must inherit.

Every row starts by copying the previous row's handoff line. Do not rewrite it. That one habit is the
entire product; the other 40 pages are there so the habit survives a bad night.

What's in the folder:

1. The 43-page PDF, print-ready, A4. Read it, scribble on it.
2. The same seven documents as editable Markdown, because you write in Markdown too.
3. Character sheet, location sheet with a floor plan, the six-shot table in three panels, a 15-item preflight
   you can run in a minute, and a drift log.
4. One complete worked example. Invented scene, invented radio host, six connected shots, one intentional
   change traced from the shot that starts it to the shot that ends it. The example is invented and the book
   says so on the colophon.
5. A CSV tracker and an offline HTML form if you'd rather type than write.

What it is not: not a prompt pack, not a model integration, no footage, no images, no promise that your

## 4. Two ways to price this, honestly compared

**Model A — free for everyone, "buy me a coffee" link only.**
- What you get: maximum goodwill, zero friction, and *some* money if the thing is good.
- What you learn: almost nothing about price. A tip jar measures generosity, not demand.
- Risk: you still won't know if $29 is real, and the next thing has the same problem.

**Model B — free for the room, $29 for strangers (recommended).**
- What you get: your people stay yours; the price is tested on people with no reason to be kind.
- What you learn: whether strangers will pay $29 for a PDF about continuity. That's the actual question.
- Risk: fewer buyers at first, because you're only selling outside. That's fine — you need 3, not 300.
- Bonus: someone in the room *will* pay anyway. Your first-ever sale will probably come from a person you
  gave it to for free, voluntarily. That's a better first sale than a cold checkout.

**Model C — $29 for everyone, including the room.**
- Honest, but it puts a gate in front of people who came for the vase. If you do this, lead the post with the
  price and a refund, and expect a few people to quietly go cold. Not wrong — just the most expensive in
  trust, and you don't need to spend that on your own room.

**One line to decide:** who is the $29 for? If the answer is "strangers, so I can learn", Model B. If it's
"everyone, because that's what it's worth", Model C with a refund.

## 7. Skool mechanics, since the editor fights you

- Paste plain text. **`#` and `**` show up literally**, as you know. Bold has to be applied with the editor's
  button, not typed.
- Use numbers for lists and a blank line between paragraphs. Two-line paragraphs beat five-line blocks.
- The `&amp;` in your last post came from pasting from something that escaped the ampersand. Type a plain `&`
  or paste into a plain-text field first.
- Put the free/paid split in the first 5 lines. Skool truncates long posts in the feed.
- One post. When people ask in the comments, answer there — that's how the post stays alive without you
  bumping it.
- Pin it. Members find pins months later; that's where the second wave of downloads comes from.
- Don't cross-post the same text to three communities in one day. Two is fine if you reword the opening.
