# Worked example

*“Night Signal”, scene 14: six connected shots, filled in, with the one change traced end to end*

**The Continuity Bible** · v1.0 · November 2026
Part 7 of 7 · Worked example. Fictional film, fictional generation log. Nothing here was shot or tested with other people.

---

One original six-shot sequence, planned with the sheets, with every field filled in. Read it once before you fill in your own scene: the fastest way to learn a template is to watch it used badly in a film you never have to make.

> **Note — What this is, and is not**  
> “Night Signal” is a fictional short invented for this product, and the drift log below is an illustration of what a working log looks like — not a record of a real generation session, a real shoot, or testing with other filmmakers. No footage exists.

# The scene

|  |  |
|---|---|
| Film | Night Signal |
| Scene | Scene 14 — “The Third Caller” |
| Logline | A late-night radio host takes a call from someone who describes the room she is sitting in. She keeps the show on air for eleven more seconds, then takes it off. |
| Time | 02:40–02:52, deep night, no windows in the room |
| Why this scene | It is one room, one on-screen character and one continuous time band, so every continuity decision is visible. It also contains the three kinds of change a real sequence usually needs: a wardrobe change, a prop state change (a spill) and a lighting change (going off air). |

## Six beats, before any field is filled in

Step one is always the beats, because the table is only as good as the shots it describes. These are written as beats, not prompts: what happens, in order.

| Shot | Slug | Length | The beat |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ROOM, FIRST LIGHT | 6 s | The room and her, mid-show. The anchor shot: it proves the room, the light and the wardrobe at once. |
| 2 | CUE SHEET, PENCIL DOWN | 5 s | She underlines a line on the cue sheet and keeps talking. |
| 3 | INSERT, CUE SHEET AND MUG | 4 s | Her hands only: the pencil, the paper, the mug lifted and set back down. |
| 4 | OVER HER SHOULDER TO THE DOOR | 5 s | She stops talking. Her head turns toward the door. |
| 5 | THE CALLER, CLOSE | 8 s | Thirty seconds later. She is listening. The mug is on its side. |
| 6 | OFF AIR | 7 s | She presses one button, leans back, and looks at the door. The lamp goes dark. |

Two decisions are made here, before anything is generated. First, every camera will stay on the south side of the desk, so the six shots cut together as one space. Second, the one change the scene needs — the spill — happens in the thirty seconds between shot 4 and shot 5, where nobody has to generate it.

## The anchor line

This is the line that goes at the start of every prompt for this character, before the action:

```
CH-01 RAE — brow scar, right · mole, left cheek · silver temples, bob tucked right ear only · he
avy close-set brows · weight on left forearm. Wearing V1: charcoal grey fine-knit cardigan butto
ned to the third button, cream crew-neck tee at the neckline, sleeves at the wrists. Matte black
 broadcast headphones on both ears, coiled cable over the right shoulder. No glasses.
```

> **Tip — Notice what the anchor line does not contain**  
> No mood, no lighting, no camera, no story. Those change per shot. Everything in this line must survive every shot, so it is kept to things that cannot quietly be different in shot 5 without you noticing.

# The change, end to end

One intentional change, three consequences, traced from the shot that starts it to the shot that ends it.

In shot 4 she hears something and turns to the door. Thirty seconds pass off-screen. When we cut back in shot 5, three things are different, and each one is a code in the ledger:

| Code | What it changes | Starts | Traced through | Ends | Why it is safe to generate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| V2 | Cardigan open, sleeves pushed to the elbows. | Shot 5 | Panel A (variant column, shots 5–6); Panel B (shot 5 changes); Panel C (shot 5 action) | Scene 15 | It is a silhouette change: open front, rolled sleeves, a bigger block of cream tee. Visible at medium and wide. |
| P-01b | Mug on its side, 20 cm coffee ring on the blotter. | Shot 5 | Prop register; Panel C (shots 5–6); the watch-out “the mug must not stand up again” | Scene 15 | The state is described as an object lying in a specific place, not as an animation. Nothing has to be spilled on camera. |
| L2 | ON AIR lamp dark, corridor strip lit under the door. | Shot 6 | Light register; Panel A (light column, shot 6); Panel B (shot 6 changes) | Until she is back on air | It is a switch, described as what is off. Dark is easier to generate than dark-and-red. |

### How the handoff is written at the seam

The seam is where a change usually leaks. Here is the exact pair of cells that closes it — the last line of shot 4 and the first line of shot 5:

```
SHOT 4 · hands off  →  “…30 seconds later, off-screen: mug tipped (P-01b) with the coffee ring on
                        the blotter, cardigan open with sleeves pushed to the elbow (V2). Lamp still L1.”

SHOT 5 · changes    →  “C — V2 (cardigan open, sleeves to the elbow). C — P-01b (mug tipped, coffee ring
                        on the blotter). Both caused by the spill during the 30-second ellipsis, both logged
                        before generating.”
```

Shot 4 names the change before it happens, so nobody has to infer it from the picture. Shot 5 names the cause, so the change reads as a decision rather than a mistake. If the two cells disagreed — the classic version is a mug that is tipped in shot 5 and upright in shot 6 — the ledger would show the contradiction in one glance, which is the entire point of writing it down.

> **Rule — The seam rule**  
> A change must be named in the shot *before* it appears, or in the shot where it appears, with a cause. Never in neither.

# Six things this example shows

1. **The anchor shot does the heavy lifting.** Shot 1 proves the room, the light and the wardrobe at once, and every later row is checked against it rather than against memory.
2. **Most rows say “none”.** Four of six changes cells are “none”, and that is what a controlled sequence looks like. A ledger full of changes is a scene that will drift.
3. **The change is authorised before it is seen.** Shot 4 hands off V2 and P-01b before shot 5 exists, so there is nothing to infer at the seam.
4. **Changes live in ellipses.** The spill is described, not animated. The hardest thing in the scene is the one nobody has to generate.
5. **Not applicable is a decision.** The insert has no face, so its anchor column says what replaces the face checks: the ring, the hand, the pencil.
6. **The bookend test is free.** Shot 6 uses shot 1's framing exactly. If the room reads as the same room at both ends, the audience believes the middle.

## Your turn, in three steps

Take a scene you already have six loose shots for, and do only this much:

1. Write the six beats. No prompts.
2. Fill the five anchors and the three never-change items for your character, then write the anchor line as one sentence.
3. Fill Panel B only — inherits, changes, hands off — for all six shots. If the middle column has more than two changes in it, you have found where your drift will come from.

That is about twenty minutes, and it is the part of this system that pays for itself first. The character and location sheets get better as you use them; the ledger is the piece that changes how the shots come out.

## Where to go next

1. **Run the preflight on your next shot.** Fifteen checks, one minute. It is the part of the system that survives a busy day.
2. **Add a second character sheet** if your scene has two people on screen, and add two columns to the table — one per character. The method does not change; only the width does.
3. **Copy the spreadsheet tracker** from the folder if you would rather work in rows than on paper. It holds the same fields as the three panels, in one wide table.
4. **Keep the drift log.** After two or three scenes you will know your own failure patterns, and those are worth more than any general advice about continuity.

> **Note — One last thing**  
> This system is a record of decisions, and its value comes from being true to the frames you actually keep. If you only use one part of it, use the inheritance ledger: a shot that starts from the previous shot's handoff line will cut with it far more often than one that starts from a fresh description.
